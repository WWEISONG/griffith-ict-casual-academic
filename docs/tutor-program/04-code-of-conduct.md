# 4. Code of conduct for casual academics

*School of ICT, Griffith University*

This sits **under** the Griffith University Code of Conduct, which applies to all
staff and is not repeated here. What follows is the specific guidance for
someone teaching in an ICT tutorial or lab — much of it addressing situations
that are common for casual academics and rarely covered anywhere.

---

## 1. You represent the School

To the students in your lab, you are Griffith. Most of them will speak to you
more often than to the convenor, and far more often than to anyone senior. What
you say about the course, the assessment or the University carries weight you
may not intend.

If you disagree with something — an assessment design, a policy, a decision —
raise it with the convenor. Not with the students.

---

## 2. Teach them to be capable

The single principle underneath the rest of this document.

A student who leaves your lab with working code they cannot explain has been
failed by you, however grateful they seemed. Your job is not to unblock them; it
is to make them someone who can unblock themselves.

In practice: hands off their keyboard, ask before you tell, and be willing to
leave someone productively stuck for a few minutes.

---

## 3. Academic integrity

**You will see it.** Copied work, shared solutions, and code the student cannot
explain.

- **Do not accuse.** It is not your call to make and getting it wrong causes
  real harm.
- **Do not ignore it either.** Report it to the convenor with what you observed
  — plainly and factually.
- **Generated code** is now the common case. The test is not whether a tool was
  used; it is whether the student understands what they submitted. If they
  cannot explain their own submission, that is what you report.
- **Do not provide solutions** to assessed work, including to a student who asks
  "informally". If you are unsure where the line is, ask the convenor before
  the student asks you.

---

## 4. Boundaries

You are close in age and often in social circles to the students you teach. That
makes boundaries harder and more necessary, not less.

- **Keep the relationship professional and visible.** Course channels and your
  Griffith email — not private messages, not personal accounts.
- **Social media.** You are not obliged to accept requests from students you
  teach, and declining is a reasonable professional position. If you are already
  connected, tell the convenor.
- **Conflicts of interest.** If you are asked to mark a friend, a partner, a
  housemate, or anyone you have a relationship with outside the course, say so
  immediately and ask for a swap. This protects you as much as them.
- **Never meet a student alone off campus** in connection with the course.
- **Never accept gifts or payment** from a student, in any form.

---

## 5. Students in difficulty

You will be told things you did not expect. Students disclose to tutors because
tutors are approachable — which is a compliment and a responsibility.

- **Listen, and do not counsel.** You are not trained for it, and it is not your
  role.
- **Refer.** Griffith provides free counselling, health, financial and academic
  support. Know where they are and say so plainly: *"There's a service for this,
  and it's free and confidential — here's the link."*
- **Escalate anything involving safety immediately** — to the convenor, the
  School office, or emergency services if the risk is immediate.
- **Do not carry it alone.** Tell the convenor that a conversation happened.
  You do not have to repeat the details.

---

## 6. Confidentiality

- **Grades, feedback and personal circumstances are confidential.** Not to be
  discussed with other students, and not outside the teaching team.
- **Return work privately.** Never read a grade aloud or leave marked work where
  others can see it.
- **Student data stays in University systems.** Do not copy submissions or
  contact lists to personal devices or accounts.
- **This survives your engagement.** It continues to apply after the trimester
  ends and after you stop tutoring.

---

## 7. Reliability

- **Be there, and be there early.** Ten minutes before, so the room and machines
  work.
- **If you cannot attend, tell the convenor as early as you can.** A session
  covered is a minor problem; a session nobody attends is a serious one.
- **Mark to the deadline you were given.** Students plan their study around
  feedback arriving.
- **Reply to student email within two working days**, even if the reply is "I'm
  looking into it".

---

## 8. Inclusion

Your lab contains international students, students with disability, students who
are the first in their family at university, and students who are quietly
certain they do not belong in computing.

- **Assume nothing about prior knowledge.** "Obviously" and "just" are words
  that tell a struggling student they are the only one.
- **Speak clearly, and be patient with questions in a second language.**
- **Distribute your attention.** The confident student who asks constantly will
  otherwise absorb the session; the student who has not spoken may need you
  most.
- **Accessibility adjustments are not optional and not yours to assess.** If a
  student mentions an adjustment, refer it to the convenor.

---

## 9. Ask when you do not know

**"I don't know — I'll find out and tell you next week"** is a professional
answer. It is also true more often than new tutors admit.

Guessing at an answer, or bluffing about assessment requirements, causes more
damage than the pause of admitting the limit. Students trust a tutor who is
straight with them.

Ask the convenor. That is what they are for.
